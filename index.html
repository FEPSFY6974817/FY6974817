Conference Paper: The Kessler Syndrome

Abstract

The Kessler syndrome, first described by Kessler and Cour-Palais (1979), describes a potential chain reaction between objects within the Earth’s orbit that generate debris, increasing the chances of further collisions in future which raise concerns for the safety of future space activities and missions. Here we discover the relationship between satellite congestion and the risks of the Kessler Syndrome whilst laying out the importance of orbital traffic coordination, satellite end-of-life disposal, and how aerospace engineers can integrate reliable spacecraft designs to mitigate space clutter, which can include redirecting debris effectively back to the Earth in safe ways. This article argues that growth in space activity must be accompanied by stronger engineering practices and international cooperation to preserve and sustain a clean and running orbital environment, as well as creating chance and potential to explore more in space.

Introduction

  When describing the Earth’s orbit as increasingly important for modern technology including communication/ navigational systems and more, it is important that we consider the degree of populating satellites and disregard objects left in orbit which has created problems for long-term sustainability of space activities which could hinder the chances for spacecrafts to go further than our GEO, Geostationary Earth Orbit, safely in the future. Space debris includes non-functional satellites such as NASA’s SEASAT launched in 1978, fragments from space collisions and discarded rocket components. These objects can travel at high speeds, making collisions a serious risk to operating spacecrafts, but can aid scientists and engineers when designing spacecraft that can filter through these problems. 
Moreso, the potential consequences of this problem were explored by scientists Donald J. Kessler and Burton G. Cour-Palais (1978), who described how collisions between artificial scientists could generate debris that increases the likelihood of further collisions, a process narrowed down as the Kessler Syndrome. In essence, this article investigates how aerospace engineering strategies can reduce the risk of the Kessler syndrome, focusing on debris prevention, collision avoidance and active debris removal.

Analysis and Discussion

  The Kessler syndrome develops when popularity of objects in Earth’s orbit becomes high enough to increase the likelihood of collisions between satellites and existing space debris. Merely, when two object collide they can break into thousands of smaller fragments which continue travelling around Earth at extremely high speeds which can hinder potential spacecraft launches or spacecraft already in Earth’s orbit. According to Kessler and Cour-Palais (1978), this process could develop into a chain reaction, where collisions produce more and more debris heightening the probability of further collisions; a collision cascade, The European Space Agency (ESA) explains that this risk is particularly significant in heavily populated orbital regions where atmospheric drag is insufficient to remove debris quickly.
An explicit important concern to raise is that this process could continue even if new satellites stopped being launched, as existing objects would still be capable of colliding. Hence, the Kessler Syndrome presents a long-term challenge to the safety and sustainability of Earth’s environment. 
Why the Kessler Syndrome Matters to Aerospace Engineering
“Micrometeoroids and orbital debris (MMOD) are the number one risk for NASA’s human spaceflight programs.” – Daniel Hoffpauir on NASA (2016)
 Whilst space debris threatens the safety and reliability of satellite and spacecraft, the Kessler Syndrome has and continues to be a major concern for aerospace engineering. Satellites are essential for everyday services such as GPS navigation, weather forecasting, television and global communications. 
The problem creates additional challenges to for aerospace engineers who must consider debris risks when designing and operating spacecraft which includes protective shielding such as the go-to-solution Whipple Shield when dealing with untraceable orbital debris invented by astronomer Fred Whipple in the 1940s, operational collision-avoidance manoeuvres (CAMs) to prevent compactness of debris on orbit such as decommissioning spacecraft to Point Nemo, spacecraft material such as using aluminium which burns off quickly, and collision monitoring. But all may exceed budgets and increase company costs as well as complexity. 
Controlling orbital debris is essential to protecting current space technology and ensuring access to Earth’s orbit.
